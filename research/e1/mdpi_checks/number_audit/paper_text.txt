ABSTRACT Courts, records offices, and newsrooms redact images with solid boxes, which hide pixels but
not their size and position (geometry). Width is known to narrow guesses, and constant-size redaction was
proposed in 2005; we quantify this side channel in raster images. We rendered 3.8 million redactions of
known text and compared exact boxes, tile-grid snapping (used by verifiable-redaction tools), and fixed-
width padding by bits leaked, top-ten recovery, and blacked-out area. For an attacker knowing the font and
text start, an exact box leaked 6.66 of the 12.88 bits in a hidden surname; with a matching frequency prior (a
best case), it was in the top ten guesses 50.2% of the time (no box: 5.95%). A 16-pixel grid left 2.19 bits at
2.45 times the area. Padding the width to a multiple of 128 pixels and the height to the full text line left 0.26
bits at 3.36 times the area, and 0.08 bits when placed at the text start; for surnames and words, no grid leaked
fewer bits at equal or lower area. Full-line height carries much of this gain: padding the width alone left
1.4 to 2.3 bits for surnames, while width-only grids covering the full line also did well. Differently aligned
grid copies nearly doubled 16-pixel hit rates; shifted copies of one padded mask added nothing, but jitter,
re-rendering, and class boundaries restored leakage. A small language model found many words from the
sentence alone. Mock forms gave similar rates; 11,351 real boxes were analysed for geometry only.
INDEX TERMS Image privacy, information leakage, measurement, redactable signatures, redaction.

I. INTRODUCTION
REDACTED images are released every day. Court
filing rules require specified personal identifiers to be
omitted or partly redacted before filing [1], and the medical
imaging standard DICOM requires burned-in identifying
text to be removed by changing the stored pixels, not by an
overlay [2]. Blur and pixelation are no longer safe: blurred
and mosaicked text can be recovered [3], and blurred faces
can be re-identified [4], [5]. Practice has therefore largely
settled on the solid black box.
A solid box hides its pixels but not its geometry: its
position, width, and height. The text around it also tells an
attacker the font, the size, and the sentence. Many systems
do not draw exact boxes either. Tools that let a third party
verify a redacted image sign the image in square blocks of
pixels, called tiles, and can only black out whole tiles [6]–[9].
They round each box outward to the tile grid, which we call
snapping. Snapping hides fine geometry but blacks out more
than was asked for, which we call over-redaction. The grid
size is therefore a dial between privacy and how much of the
page stays readable.

It has long been known that the width of a redacted word
narrows the candidates [10]; constant-size redaction, extra
blacked-out words, and jiggling nearby text were proposed
against it in 2005 [11], and leftover glyph positions have
been used to break PDF redactions [12]. We measure, in bits,
how much of the leak from box geometry survives snapping
and padding in raster images, and what each setting costs
in blacked-out area. In short, an exact box made a hidden
surname about eight times easier to guess than no box at
all, and even a 64-pixel (px) grid still leaked 0.67 bits and
blacked out about ten times the text area.
A. THE RESEARCH GAP
The leak itself is known (Section II-C). To our knowledge,
no study has measured in bits how much a solid box’s size
and position reveal in raster images with known hidden text,
or how much snapping or fixed-width padding reduces that
leak and at what cost in blacked-out area on the same boxes.
Verifiable-redaction papers prove that hidden pixels stay
hidden but treat the box itself as public. Our contribution is
the measurement, not width-based attacks or constant-size
redaction [10], [11] (Table I).
B. RESEARCH QUESTIONS
RQ1 How much does a solid box’s geometry reveal about
the hidden text, to attackers of increasing strength
and with imperfect knowledge?
RQ2 How does snapping to a grid (exact, 8, 16, 32,
or 64 px), random jitter, or fixed-width padding
trade that leak against over-redaction on the same
boxes, and how does the leak compose over several
releases?
RQ3 What do real redaction boxes look like, and what
would each grid hide and cost for them?
The study plan stated the expected order of leakage and cost
in advance (Appendix C).
C. CONTRIBUTIONS
• C1 (RQ1): a posterior-entropy measurement, in bits
under a stated side-information model, of what raster
box geometry leaks to four attackers, one using a small
language model, over five kinds of text, six fonts,
four sizes, and three image conditions, with font-level
uncertainty, imperfect-knowledge attackers, and 200
mock forms from two renderers (Sections III–IV-G).
• C2 (RQ2): a paired privacy–area evaluation on the
same boxes of grids, jitter, and fixed-width padding,
including the padding class, placement, and height,
ink boxes, width-only grids, the language model, and
releases of several differently aligned or re-rendered
copies (Sections IV-B–IV-D).
• C3 (RQ3): a geometry calibration on 11,351 real
redactions: what each grid would hide and cost for
them. The hidden text of these boxes is unknown and
was not recovered (Section IV-H).
Snapping was modelled on, and checked against, TileSeal,
an open tool for signed, redactable images built on a standard
construction [13], [14]. We claim no new cryptography
(Appendix D).
II. STATE OF THE ART
A. ATTACKS ON REDACTION
The two closest studies [10], [12] are compared with ours
in Section II-C. RedactBuster predicts the type of a redacted
entity from its sentence [15], and Charpentier and Lison
recovered up to 80% of de-identified text spans with a
language model and background knowledge [16]. Pixel-
level attacks break mosaics and blur [3]; most tested face
anonymisations are at least partly reversible [4], and heavily
blurred faces were re-identified in 95.9% of closed-world
cases, whose authors recommend a full black mask [5].
The attack we model is now available as public tools.
Unredact filters a dictionary by box width and font metrics
and ranks the survivors with a language model [17]; Deredact
decodes Llama 2 under a character-count limit [18], [19];
and a PDF checker flags redactions whose size leaks [20].
Rasterised bars over monospaced text in the released Epstein
PDFs still revealed character counts [21], and a TUGboat
workflow removes sensitive values from the PDF text
layer [22]. Reasoning models strengthen re-identification of
masked text [23], and LeakageBench measures personal data
that redaction tools miss [24]. None of these measures in
bits what box geometry reveals, or tests snapping or padding
against its cost. For encrypted messages, length-hiding
padding likewise trades leakage against overhead under an
application’s message distribution [25].
B. VERIFIABLE REDACTION AND PROVENANCE
Redactable signatures [13], [14], [26] let anyone remove parts
of a signed message and keep the rest verifiable, in the sim-
plest version by signing salted hashes of the parts. For images
this appears as signed tile hashes [6], [7], croppable JPEG
signatures on 8-pixel blocks [8], and PhotoAssurance [27].
Zero-knowledge (ZK) proofs show that an image was edited
correctly without revealing the original [28], [29]; VIMz
includes a 32-pixel block-redaction circuit [9], and Trustless
Provenance redacts at pixel precision [30]. In the standard
of the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity
(C2PA), ‘‘redaction’’ removes assertions, and blacking out
pixels needs a new manifest and signer [31]; Golaszewski
et al. found that its exclusion range enabled undetectable
alterations in version 2.2 [32], and version 2.4 requires that
excluded data cannot change how the asset is interpreted [31].
Provenance can also be defeated by photographing a
displayed fake, which Scoop detects [33]. Van Heusden et al.
released annotated redactions from freedom-of-information
pages [34], [35], used in Section IV-H.

III. METHODOLOGY
The study has five steps: render known text in a given font
and size; draw the box a redactor would draw; apply a
redaction policy (exact box, grid snapping, jitter, or fixed-
width padding); let attackers with different knowledge list
and rank every candidate that fits the box; and measure how
often the truth is near the top, how many bits the box gave
away, and how much extra area was blacked out.
A. THREAT MODEL AND ATTACKERS
A custodian releases an image in which a solid box covers
some text. The requested box is the text box reported by the
layout engine (Pillow’s getbbox) plus 1 px on each side. Its
right, top, and bottom edges follow the ink, but its left edge is
the start of the text (the pen position) whenever the first glyph
starts to the right of it, as almost all do, so it carries little
first-letter detail. We call this the layout box; Section IV-B
also uses the ink box, which hugs the inked pixels (plus 1 px).
Every attacker knows the font, the size, the kind of text, and
its prior, that is, how common each entry in the list is. The
attacker keeps the candidate set: the entries whose rendered
and redacted box would match the published one. It then
ranks them by probability given the box (Section III-D); for
A1 this is the prior. We use four attackers:
• A1, start known: knows where the hidden text starts,
from the visible text before it, and the baseline; a candi-
date must reproduce all four box edges relative to them.
• A2, width only: knows the baseline but not the start, and
keeps a candidate if any start reproduces the box.
• A3, several copies: holds k ∈ {1, 2, 3, 5}copies of
the release, each shifted by a random 0–63 px so that
each snaps differently. It knows the shifts but not the
start. It ran on surnames and words in DejaVu Sans and
Liberation Serif at 16 px (n = 4,000 per row). With
k=1 it equals A2.
• A4, language model: ranks A1’s candidate set with
Qwen2.5-0.5B, a small open language model, with
no retrieval or background knowledge. It scores each
candidate on the words before the gap, then re-scores
the top 30 with the whole sentence. Its test set was 400
words from sentences in eight public-domain books,
each at least 3 letters long and outside the 100 most
frequent words, rendered at 16 px on clean images in
DejaVu Sans and, in a second run, Liberation Serif. As
a control, the same model ranked the whole list with no
box, in the same two steps.
B. DATA AND RENDERING
There were five kinds of hidden text, each with a stated prior
and its entropy:
• the 50,000 most common US surnames in title case,
weighted by count (12.88 bits); the cut-off falls inside
a tie at count 419, broken by census file order [36];
• the 20,000 most frequent alphabetic English words, in
lower case (10.10 bits) [37];
• digit strings of 4–10 digits (26.06 bits);
• dates from 1940–2025 in two formats (15.94 bits);
• e-mail addresses built from the 1,000 most common
surnames, with five webmail domains (15.61 bits).
Text was rendered black on white on a single line with the
Pillow imaging library (FreeType, RAQM layout) in six fonts:
DejaVu Sans and Serif, Liberation Sans, Serif, and Mono,
and Noto Sans. Sizes were 12, 16, and 24 px, plus 42 px
(about 10-point text at 300 DPI) for surnames, words, and
e-mails in DejaVu Sans and Liberation Sans and Serif. The
start position and baseline were uniform over the page. There

C. REDACTION POLICIES
Grid snapping. A tool with grid G publishes the box
[⌊x0/G⌋G, ⌈x1/G⌉G) ×[⌊y0/G⌋G, ⌈y1/G⌉G), that is, the
requested box rounded outward to the grid. G=1 is an exact
tool, such as an image editor or a pixel-exact ZK system;
G ∈ {8, 16, 32, 64}covers tile schemes, including 32-px
ZK block circuits. Jitter: each side of the box is extended
by a random 0–16 px before snapping. Fixed-width padding:
the width is rounded up to a multiple of 128 px, which we
call its class, keeping the box’s left edge (edge-aligned),
and the height is padded to the full text line, from the font’s
ascent above the baseline to its descent below it (Pillow’s
font metrics). A later sweep added classes of 32–256 px,
start-aligned padding (left edge at a fixed offset from the
text start), a 128-px grid, and width-only grids (only the side
edges snapped, full-line height); there the line was widened
to cover every list entry’s box, plus 1 px. A last check kept the
box’s own height (box-height padding). Padding is the same
every time for the same text, unlike jitter. Both defences were
applied by our harness to the requested box before redaction,
so neither needs a change to the tool.
The tool. TileSeal [38] cuts the image into 64×64 tiles, or
8-, 16-, or 32-px sub-tiles, records a salted SHA-256 hash of
each with its position, and signs all hashes with one Ed25519
signature. Anyone can redact without a key. Box geometry
is not hidden. In all 392 test redactions, our model of snap-
ping predicted exactly which tiles TileSeal blacked out (Ap-
pendix C). At that commit TileSeal had no padding option.
D. MEASURES
Top-10: how often the truth is among the attacker’s ten best
guesses (a hit); ties are counted in expectation over a random
tie-break, so a truth tied across tenth place is a fractional
hit. We give differences in percentage points (‘‘points’’).
Length accuracy: how often the attacker’s most likely length
is right. Over-redaction: the blacked-out area divided by the
requested area, summarised by its median over boxes. The
no-box rate is what the prior alone gives.

Leakage is thus a property of a box under a stated side-
information model, not of the box alone. On degraded
images the attacker keeps candidates within a calibrated edge
tolerance, weighted by the prior; those bits, and those for digit
strings (classes with one representative box), are model-based
tolerance-set values, not mutual information. A truth outside
the set counts as a miss, with the entropy taken over the set
(the whole list if it is empty); on clean images this occurred
only for digits, in at most 0.9% of a cell. Bits and top-10
answer different questions. Bits say how much the box shrinks
the candidate list under the stated prior. Top-10 says how
often the truth ends up near the top, so it also depends on how
well the attacker’s ranking matches that prior (Section IV-E).
E. STATISTICS
Each cell (one kind of text, font, size, condition, grid,
and attacker) has 2,000 targets drawn from the prior with
replacement (a common surname can appear twice), under a
seed of its own for each font and size. The same targets and
positions are reused for every condition, grid, defence,
and attacker, so comparisons are paired. Main results
pool six fonts and three sizes (n = 36,000 per cell).
These 36,000 boxes are not independent: results differ
between fonts. We therefore report 95% font-level bootstrap
confidence intervals (CIs), which resample the six fonts with
replacement (10,000 resamples), and the range across fonts.
For a difference we take it within each font and resample
the fonts. These intervals describe sensitivity over these six
fixed fonts, not fonts in general; they are per comparison
and uncorrected. The planned Holm correction over one-
sided two-proportion tests of top-10 in the 18 font–size cells
(w9_holm_summary.csv) kept exact > 8 > 16 > 32 px
in all 18 cells for surnames and words, 32 > 64 px in 10 and
9, and padding below 64 px in 2 and 3. Smaller tests (A3, A4,
mock forms) give intervals over items, stated in each caption.

IV. RESULTS
A. HOW MUCH BOXES LEAK (RQ1)
Exact boxes leaked heavily (Table II). A1, which knows
where the text starts, put the hidden surname in its top
ten 50.2% of the time (font-level CI 41.4–57.2%) and first
20.4%, against 5.95% and 0.97% with no box. For common
words the figure was 79.7%, against 23.8%. In bits, exact
boxes leaked 6.66 (surnames) and 6.91 (words). The font
mattered a lot: exact-box surname hits ranged from 30.2%
in the monospaced Liberation Mono, where width mostly
reflects length, to 61.1% in DejaVu Serif. Knowing the start
mattered little for exact boxes (A2: 49.0% and 79.0%).
Snapping reduced the leak as the grid grew, but never
removed it. Surname hits fell to 14.9% at 16 px (CI 14.2–
15.5%) and 8.2% at 64 px. Snapping also narrowed the
differences between fonts: at 16 px surname hits ranged only
from 13.7% to 15.8%. Digit strings were never guessed,
but their length leaked: the most likely length was right for
68.4% of 16-px boxes, against 14.3% with no box. E-mail
addresses behaved like surnames, at a lower level: 18.2%
top-10 for exact boxes and 3.8% at 16 px, against 1.24% with
no box. Image quality helped only for exact boxes. Scan-like
images cut exact-box surname hits from 50.2% to 27.9%, but
at 16 px only from 14.9% to 12.9%. Larger text leaked more
for exact and 16-px boxes: at 42 px, exact boxes gave 66.9%
for surnames and 87.5% for words (Table VIII); at 64 px the
change was small and not monotone (surnames 7.9, 7.6, and
9.2% at 12, 16, and 24 px).
B. GRIDS, JITTER, AND PADDING (RQ2)
Fig. 1 plots each policy’s leak against its cost. The grid points
form a curve that flattens as the grid grows: median over-
redaction for surnames grew from 1.60×at 8 px to 10.04×at
64 px, because a tile is large compared with the height of a text
box. The padding points sit below and to the left of that curve.
The tested 128-px padding setting gave a favourable
leakage–area trade-off against the tested grids (Fig. 1). For
surnames it leaked 0.26 bits and reached 7.3% top-10 at
3.36×over-redaction, against 0.67 bits and 8.2% for a 64-px
grid at 10.04×; it also leaked fewer bits than the 32-px grid at
less area. Compared font by font, padding leaked fewer bits
than the 64-px grid in all six fonts, by 0.41 bits on average
(font-level CI 0.25–0.55; target-level paired CI 0.39–0.42),
and 0.37 to 0.47 bits with any one font left out (Table IX).
Its top-10 was lower in four of six fonts, by 0.96 points on
average (CI 0.05–1.83; target-level 0.80–1.13). That gap is
small; the case for padding rests on bits and area. For words,
padding also leaked fewer bits than a 64-px grid in every
font. Padding with a 16-px grid left almost nothing, at more
area. Random jitter alone behaved like a 16-px grid (2.09
against 2.19 bits for surnames).
These padding results use the layout box, whose left edge
rarely moves with the first letter, and padding keeps that
edge. A later sweep on the published targets (all five lists,
six fonts, three sizes, A1 and A2, clean; Table III) added ink
boxes, classes of 32–256 px in both placements, a 128-px
grid, and width-only grids; it reproduced all 432 published
layout-box cells it repeated. With ink boxes, edge-aligned
padding shows the first letter: for surnames it leaked 0.97
bits, more than the 64-px grid (0.68), and more in 13 of 18
font–size cells. Start-aligned padding removed this: 0.08
bits at 3.36×(top-10 6.2%) for either box and attacker. For
surnames and words it leaked fewer bits than every 8–64-px
grid in all 18 font–size cells, and no grid, the 128-px and
width-only grids included, leaked fewer bits than any start-
aligned class at up to 10% more area, pooled or in any cell
(except 3 of 72 cells at 32 px, A2). A start-aligned box shows
where the text starts, so A2 learns as much as A1; against
A2, width-only 64- and 128-px grids (0.25 and 0.06 bits at
3.01×and 4.56×) lie between the start-aligned points.
The class must cover the field. For surnames and words,
about eight times the text size (96, 128, and 192 px at 12, 16,
and 24 px) left at most 0.005 bits; narrower classes leak at
their boundaries. For e-mail addresses, 128-px start-aligned
padding leaked 0.56 bits, less than the 64-px grid for A1
(0.71) but more for A2 (0.32), where a width-only 64-px grid
did better at the same area (0.31 bits); a 192-px class left 0.18
bits, yet at 24 px a 256-px class leaked 0.73. For dates, start-
aligned 128-px padding leaked less than every 8–64-px grid in
15–17 of 18 cells for A1 but in 10–11 for A2. Digit strings are
omitted, as this sweep scores them with grouped candidates.
Full-line height carries much of padding’s gain. A further
check on the same targets (surnames and words, six fonts,
three sizes, A1 and A2, exact likelihood; w1_pooled.csv)
padded only the width and kept the box’s own height (box-
height rows in Table III); the height of a tight box shows
which ascenders and descenders the text has. At 128 px this
left 1.57 bits for surnames (A1, layout boxes; top-10 12.1%),
1.43 start-aligned, and 2.27 edge-aligned on ink boxes, at
2.29×, and 2.04–2.74 bits for words. That is close to the grids
of similar area (16-px grid: 2.19 bits at 2.45×; width-only

C. SEVERAL COPIES (A3)
Against grids, each extra copy held by A3 narrowed the
candidates (Table IV). At 16 px, surname hits rose from
11.5% with one copy to 22.0% with five (per font: 10.7–
12.4% to 20.4–23.6%), and words rose too. Five 16-px
copies leaked 3.30 bits, more than one 8-px copy for the
same attacker (2.38 bits) and more than one 8-px copy for
A1, which knows the start (3.09 bits; difference 0.21, target-
level CI 0.18–0.24, two fonts only). Even at 64 px, five copies
raised surname hits from 7.0% to 11.1%.
Against padding, copies added nothing. On the same
targets and shifts, padding kept surnames at 5.9% and words
at 24.6% for every k, the no-box level (Table IV), and the
candidate set at k=5 equalled the one at k=1 for all 8,000 tar-
gets, with or without a 16-px grid. With five copies, padding
stayed below the 64-px grid by 5.2 points and 1.21 bits for
surnames (CI−5.9 to−4.5 points) and 8.4 points and 1.62
bits for words, at about a third of the area. Each padded copy
is the same box moved by a known offset: the height is the
full line, and a 128-px class is a whole number of 16-px tiles.
At 16 px nearly every target fits in the first class, so this is a
weak test. On harder sets (e-mails at 16 px, where about 92%
of targets need more than one class, and surnames and words
at 24 px), padding again gained nothing from copies, whereas
a 16-px grid rose from 1.98 to 4.16 bits for 24-px surnames.
Padding still leaks in three ways (Table X, Appendix B).
(1) Jittering each copy by 0–16 px before padding let copies
leak again (24-px surnames: 0.28 bits with one copy, 0.57
with five). (2) The class tells the attacker which side of a
class boundary the text falls on: at 24 px, the 0.2–8.7% of
surname probability that needs a 256-px box leaks 2.5–4.7
bits when it occurs, and on lists chosen to straddle a boundary
the class gave up to 1 bit and raised top-10 by up to 22 points.
(3) Releasing the same surnames again in another font, size,
or renderer changed the class for 171 of 2,000 surnames at
24 px; those leaked 4.9 bits, and overall leakage rose from
0.20 to 0.63 bits. At 16 px no class changed, but an attacker
that knew the start gained from small left-edge differences
(6.8% to 9.8%). Padding resists copies only when every
release reuses the same padded box.

D. SENTENCE CONTEXT (A4)
For words, the language model gave the highest top-10 rates
we measured (Table V). Ranking A1’s candidates, it put the
word in its top ten 97.0% of the time for exact boxes and
49.8% at 64 px, 38.5 to 72.8 points more than word frequency.
The fair comparison is with the sentence alone: with no box,
the same model reached 44.8% (CI 40.0–49.7; first 23.8%),
and 33.0% with the left context only. Against the sentence
alone, the box added 52.3 points for exact boxes, 26.0 at 16 px
(CI 21.5–30.3), and only 5.0 at 64 px (CI 3.0–7.3), and it never
lost a word the sentence alone had found. So at coarse grids
most of what this attacker recovered came from the sentence,
not the box. In Liberation Serif, model plus box was 1.0 to
4.5 points below DejaVu Sans up to 32 px and equal at 64 px
(separate sessions), and the gain at 64 px was again 5.3 points.
With padding, the box added almost nothing to the
sentence: 45.25% in DejaVu Sans and 45.0% in Liberation
Serif, against 45.0% for the sentence alone in the same rerun
(gain 0.25 points, CI 0.0–0.75, and 0.0 points; Table V),
4.25 and 4.50 points below the 64-px grid (CI−6.50 to
−2.25 and−6.75 to−2.50), at 3.76×against 10.84×over-
redaction; padding with a 16-px grid gave the same top ten.
The model’s ranks were not exactly reproducible: the rerun
matched every candidate set and prior rank but changed the
model’s rank for 14–23% of items, moving top-10 by at most
0.5 points. Multi-threaded reruns of 12 items differed and
single-threaded ones did not, pointing to multi-threaded CPU
arithmetic; we treat it as noise of about ±0.5 points.
A4 is a lower-bound contextual attacker (one small model,
no background knowledge, clean images): a small gain from
the box means the box adds little for this attacker, not that
the hidden identity is safe.

E. WHEN THE ATTACKER IS UNSURE
So far every attacker knew the font, the size, the list, and its
prior. Fig. 2 takes each away in turn; read each row against
the top row. Leakage dropped in every case. An attacker
who did not know the font and tried all six kept most of the
exact-box leak: surname hits fell from 48.6% to 38.0% (drop
10.6 points, CI 5.6–15.0), and not knowing the size as well
changed almost nothing. At 16 px, not knowing font and size
took surnames from 14.6% to 9.4% (no box: 5.95%), and bits
from 2.10 to 0.71 under an even belief over the 18 font–size
options (a model-based value, as the true size was always
16 px). An attacker sure of a wrong font or size did much
worse: for exact boxes the truth was almost never among its
candidates (surnames 0.6%), and ±2 px of slack helped little.
A shorter list, the top 5,000 surnames, cost little.
The prior mattered most. With a uniform prior over the
same list, exact-box surname hits fell from 48.6% to 5.7%.
On the published 16-px targets (s4_priors.csv), ranking
the same candidates by an independently sourced prior, each
surname’s frequency as an English word, cut exact-box hits
from 48.3% to 21.2%, and census counts shuffled across
names to 5.2%. Bits under the true prior stayed at 6.48, and
the box still narrowed the list for the misinformed attacker,
whose no-box rate was 0.03%. Top-10 is therefore a statement
about the box and the attacker’s prior together, whereas bits
describe what the geometry alone gives up.

G. A SECOND RENDERER
To check that the mock-form result does not depend on one
rendering pipeline, we built 100 new forms with the same
fields, converted them with LibreOffice 25.2 and rasterised
them at 96 DPI, in DejaVu Sans and Liberation Serif at 16
and 24 px, clean and after a simulated scan, and attacked the
surname with A1 (Table VI). With the attacker matched to
LibreOffice, exact boxes put 63 of 100 surnames in the top
ten at 16 px (73 at 24 px), against 52 in the first forms. A
16-px grid cut this by 50 points (form-paired CI 40–60), and
the 64-px grid and padding left 5–14%, against 6% with no
box. Not knowing the font cut exact-box hits to 20%, and a
uniform prior to 13%.
Renderer mismatch is itself a finding. With our Pillow
model and tolerance, the attacker found 0 of 100 exact-box
surnames on clean pages, because the two renderers’ ink
boxes differ by up to 3 px per edge. This is not protection:
calibrating the tolerance on 200 LibreOffice strings restored
31%, and after scan simulation the mismatch no longer
mattered (23% against 22%); grid results did not depend on
the renderer model (0–1 points). Padding and the 16-px grid
were close: the grid minus padding was +4 [−1, 10] and +7
[0, 14] points on clean pages at 16 and 24 px, and +5 [1, 10]
and +12 [6, 19] after scans (form-paired 95% intervals), so
padding was clearly lower only after scans. On clean pages
the 64-px grid leaked less than padding (−3 [−7, 0] and
−7 [−12,−2] points), at about five times its over-redaction
(17.6×against 3.4×at 16 px).

H. REAL REDACTION BOXES (RQ3)
Real boxes were mostly single text lines: median width
228 px, median height 49 px, and 93.6% had heights of 25–
90 px. Their widths carried 9.35 bits of entropy over 1,433
distinct values, and snapping to 64 px cut this to 3.67 bits
(Table VII) at a median over-redaction of 2.95×, lower than
in the synthetic study because real boxes are larger. Black
boxes alone gave almost the same figures. Width entropy
is a ceiling on what a width could reveal about the hidden
text, not a measured leak; the hidden text is unknown, so we
attempted no recovery.
V. DISCUSSION
A. SNAPPING ALONE IS NOT ENOUGH
For text, a grid trades leakage for area at a poor rate. Getting
close to the no-box rate took a 64-px grid and about ten
times the text area (a 128-px grid still leaked 0.33 bits for
surnames at 28.6×), and digit-string length leaked at every
grid. Verifiable-redaction schemes inherit this: tile signatures
such as TileSeal, croppable block signatures [8], and ZK
block circuits [9] publish a snapped box, and pixel-exact ZK
redaction [30] publishes the exact box, which leaked most.
As far as we can tell, none of their security definitions covers
box geometry, TileSeal’s included. Snapping also costs
readability: 16-px boxes cut into a neighbouring Notes-line
word on 94% of mock forms (LibreOffice 87%), and 64-px
boxes fully hid about one visible word in seven.
B. WHEN PADDING HOLDS, AND WHEN IT DOES NOT
For surnames and words, start-aligned padding gave the
best leakage–area trade-off we measured: with layout or ink
boxes, no tested grid leaked fewer bits at equal or lower
area (Table III); edge-aligned padding did so only with
layout boxes. Two ingredients matter. The full-line height is
load-bearing: padding the width but keeping the box’s own
height left 1.4–2.3 bits for surnames, no better than grids of
similar area, while width-only grids that black out the full
line also did well, and for e-mails against an attacker without
the start were sometimes better. And the class must cover
nearly every value of the field, about eight times the text size
for surnames and words. Padding fits any scheme, because
the padded box is simply the region that is redacted. It held
against shifted copies of one mask and against the language
model, but not against jitter, class boundaries, or re-rendering
(Section IV-C), and on clean LibreOffice forms the 64-px grid
gave a lower top-10, at five times the area (Section IV-G). The
evidence is synthetic single lines and 200 mock forms, so this
is an empirical result for our lists, renderers, and attackers,
not a general optimum. In practice: pad the width to a class
chosen for the field, black out the full line height, place the
box at a fixed offset from the text start, and reuse one mask
for every release. Where area matters little, a coarse grid or
padding with a 16-px grid leaks less; if a document must be
re-rendered, every version should black out the same region,
sized for the largest class any version needs (untested).
C. RELEASE ONCE, AND TREAT CONTEXT AS SECRET
With grids, differently aligned copies add up, so a tool should
reuse one canonical mask (the same page coordinates) for
every release; if several versions are unavoidable, each should
black out the union of all their boxes. Within one page, using
both boxes of a repeated surname raised 16-px hits from 12%
to 16% on the mock forms (a small sample). If the sentence
predicts the word, no box setting hides it: at coarse grids and
with padding, most of what the language model recovered
came from the sentence (Section IV-D), so the context should
be redacted or rewritten too. Real risk also depends on the
attacker’s prior: with a uniform prior, exact-box surname hits
fell from 48.6% to 5.7% (Section IV-E).
VI. LIMITATIONS
• Missing tests (most important first). (1) The padding
sweep and box-height check (Table III) cover clean
images, A1 and A2 only; start-aligned padding was not
tested against copies, the language model, degraded
images, or mock forms. The sweep’s digit-string rows
use grouped candidates and are not reported, and its
renderer used FriBidi 1.0.8 rather than 1.0.16, although
every published cell it repeated matched. (2) Only one
small language model was run, with no retrieval or
background knowledge. (3) Padding is not a general
bound for repeated releases: copies, a known start, and
re-rendering were tested on clean surnames and words
(and e-mails for copies) at 16 and 24 px, LibreOffice re-
rendering on surnames only, independent re-redaction
only as 0–16 px jitter, and boundary effects only on our
lists. (4) No recovery was attempted on real documents.
The mock forms are synthetic, use one layout, and num-
ber 100 per set; the LibreOffice set was attacked on sur-
names with A1 only, and both renderers use FreeType.
(5) There was no run with uniformly sampled targets,
so top-10 depends on the priors. (6) There was no face
study. (7) A3 ran on four units at 16 px plus the harder
padding sets; A4 used A1 on clean images only, and its
two fonts ran in different sessions. (8) No font or list was
held out in advance, and there was no larger held-out
font set, physical print–scan, or multi-template form set.
• Uncertainty. With six fonts, the font-level intervals are
approximate; A3, A4, and the mock forms have intervals
over items only. Each interval describes the material it

VII. CONCLUSION
A redacted word’s width was known to narrow the
candidates [10], and constant-size redaction had been
proposed [11]; we measured, in bits and on the same boxes,
how much of that leak survives in raster images under
tile snapping and fixed-width padding, and at what cost in
blacked-out area. Snapping buys little privacy for the area it
blacks out, and differently aligned copies undo much of it,
so any tool that publishes box geometry, verifiable-redaction
schemes included, should reuse one canonical mask across
releases. Padding the width to a class while blacking out the
full line height, placed at a fixed offset from the text start,
gave the best trade-off for surnames and words; without the
full-line height it did not. It holds only when every release
reuses the same unjittered mask and the class covers the field.
Guessable context has to be treated as part of what is hidden.
These conclusions rest on synthetic single-line text, mock
forms from two renderers, and the geometry of real boxes;
testing start-aligned padding on copies, degraded images,
and real documents is the most direct next step.

e: Metrics
No-box rates are the prior’s top-10 and most likely length on
the sampled targets; smaller tests carry Wilson 95% intervals.
The original code weighted every compatible candidate
equally, which is exact only for A1 without jitter; jitter, A2
and A3 at 8–64 px, jittered padding, unknown font or size,
and mock-form A2 were rescored with the exact likelihood
over every sampled start and jitter draw, and the values
rose or stayed equal (POSTERIOR_AUDIT.md). Several
releases. A3 and jittered padding use the joint likelihood: the
product of each copy’s likelihood, summed over the shared
start. In the other multi-release checks every release gives
each candidate a likelihood of 0 or one common value, so
intersecting the compatible sets is the joint likelihood. For
re-rendered releases the width-only attacker uses only each
release’s width class.

APPENDIX D THE REDACTION TOOL
TileSeal is the tool whose snapping we model. It uses a
standard signed-hash construction [13], [14]; the file format
is specified in the repository.
a: Seal, redact, verify
The image is cut into 64×64 tiles, each optionally split into
8-, 16-, or 32-px sub-tiles. For each unit the signer publishes a
SHA-256 hash of a secret salt, the unit’s position, and its pix-
els, and one Ed25519 signature covers all hashes, the image
size, and the grid. To redact, anyone snaps the box outward to
the grid, sets the covered pixels to zero, and replaces each cov-
ered unit’s salt by its hash. Verification checks the signature,
recomputes the hash of every visible unit, and requires every
hidden unit to be all zero. Without that last check, a ‘‘redac-
tion’’ could leave the original pixels under a drawn box.
b: What we did not test
The repository argues that changing what stays visible would
need an Ed25519 forgery or a SHA-256 collision, and that
salted hashes hide the covered pixels; with several differently
redacted copies, only units hidden in every copy stay hidden.
We did not evaluate these claims: they are not machine-
checked, there was no independent audit, and none covers
box geometry, which is what this paper measures.
c: Tests
At the cited commit all 119 repository tests passed, and 27
named attacks (pixel, salt, signature, and key edits, splices
across seals, a fake blackout), 10,000 seeded bit flips, and
50,002 byte mutations were rejected or handled as specified,
with no disagreement between the Python and JavaScript
verifiers. This shows resistance to these attacks, not freedom
from bugs.
ETHICS CONSIDERATIONS
The study used only synthetic or public data and recovered no
real redacted secret. All attacks ran on text that we rendered
and redacted ourselves, and the 200 mock forms hold only
invented values. From the real dataset we downloaded box
annotations only, never page images. No human participants
were involved. The attack techniques are already public [10],
[12], [16], and publishing their measured strength helps tool
